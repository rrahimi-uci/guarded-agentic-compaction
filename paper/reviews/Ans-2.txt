Response to Reviewer 2 (1/2)

Thank you for a review that names exactly what the paper had not shown. We take your two weaknesses first, then the three questions. Section, table and appendix numbers refer to the revised PDF; the September 24 version lacked the studies cited below.

W1: the workloads do not establish the practical value of automatic guarded compilation. The tasks are metadata classification and extraction, a handwritten workflow matches GAC, and the cross-repository work keeps the simpler PR-outcome task.

Answer: we agree with each of these facts, and we changed what the paper claims rather than dispute them.

- The primary tasks are metadata classification and verbatim extraction; Section 4 now says that no decision in them needs semantic judgment. The handwritten program matches GAC on contracts, 90/90 to 90/90 (Table 2), ties on requests and does somewhat better on tokens and cost (71.1% vs 63.1% and 60.4% vs 58.7% reductions, Table 14).
- The paper now states the contribution as safe automation of that program. GAC finds the program an engineer would write, from traces, without being told the workflow, and refuses where it cannot justify one. Matching the handwritten program is therefore the intended result, and the paper no longer presents it as a shortfall. The abstract, Section 1 and the conclusion carry this framing, and a new Table 1 ("What the compiler decided without human input") opens Section 5.
- Section 5 and the "Decision Audit Examples" appendix show the retained program and admission statistics, the structural reason a third read was refused, and the recorded reason an old artifact abstained on a later snapshot. These are inspectable decisions, not a measured explainability advantage over other compilers.
- Evidence at larger scale (new Appendix F.2, Table 19): eleven repositories, 3,560 fresh pull-request groups, one pull request per author, sampled before any outcome filter. The compiled, unchanged and handwritten arms meet 3,510, 3,507 and 3,504 final contracts; compiled and handwritten both cut cost by 73.6%. The guard refuses where evidence is insufficient: node and cpython are retired at n = 400. Adverse: on godot the compiled arm trails the unchanged agent, 393 vs 399 (p = 0.070).
- What the automatic decisions are worth: a four-policy funnel over all 13 retained cohorts (new Appendix N subsection) shows statistical admission never changed a compile-time decision and grounding changed one, the issue-type third read; a sealed pilot on 193 fresh issues found that refusal did not recur and that GAC, the unguarded replay of the same program and the unchanged agent met the contract on 65/65, 63/65 and 64/65. The value we can show is reproducible, inspectable refusal, not a measured quality gain.
- Discovery cost breaks even after 411, 183 and 181 episodes for the three families (Section 5.1). PR-outcome is used for the cross-repository and post-cutoff studies because it is the family with enough fresh records; those studies test transfer and certification, not task difficulty.
- Beyond metadata: BIRD text-to-SQL (Section 5.2), a live SQL agent after a compiled schema-first opening. The opening compiles on 26/31 databases. On 150 sealed questions the compiled agent answers 97 and the unchanged agent 94, with 44.9% fewer requests than a warm rerun of the unchanged agent; on a second model and on the training split it trails warm reruns of the unchanged agent (93 vs 95 of 150; 402 vs 412 of 630). BIRD shows savings at similar accuracy, not an accuracy gain. The "matches handwritten" claim is limited to GitHub: on BIRD a guarded one-call candidate trailed an exact handwritten prefetch (Appendix L).

Still open: the engineering value of automation over hand-writing (engineer time) is not quantified, and no advantage on adaptive tasks is claimed (see the third question).

Suggested additional validation: compare full GAC, unguarded replay and a handwritten or composite-tool baseline within one workflow that has both recurrent retrieval and genuinely adaptive decisions.

Answer: we did not complete this experiment and cannot before November 18, so the paper claims nothing that depends on it.

- Three attempts stopped at prespecified development gates (Appendix L). BIRD: a guarded one-call candidate answered 26/36 against 28/36 for both the unchanged agent and an exact handwritten prefetch. AppWorld: the adapted baselines scored 1/12 and 2/12, below the 8/12 competence gate, so no GAC arm ran. AgentHop: the baseline passed (19/24 against a 17/24 gate), but no grounded selector for the dependent read could be derived.
- The protocol for the full study is in the supplement (paper/supplementary/adaptive-quality-cost-study.md). We estimate 12 to 16 working days and a budget increase, and we are not promising it for this discussion.

Response to Reviewer 2 (2/2)

W2: the contribution of the guards and the learned gate is unestablished: barrier-free replay ties GAC (reviewed Table 19); disabling several barriers at once prevents attribution; the learned gate (reviewed Table 23) shows no ranking value and its comparison also changed the risk budget and grid; the drift comparison was not run.

Answer: partly agreed. The learned-gate claim is withdrawn, and the missing studies now exist, with mixed results.

- Barrier-free replay: recurrence-only replay still ties GAC at 30/30 with one fewer request on that cohort (Table 33), and the new funnel and 65-issue pilot (Appendix N) agree: grounding changed one compile-time decision in 13 cohorts, it did not recur on fresh data, and live the guarded and unguarded programs met the contract on 65/65 and 63/65. We do not count a refusal as an avoided error.
- One mechanism at a time: a live-recovery study removes request-scope admission or completeness checking one at a time over 2,304 scheduled episodes (Appendix I). The scope guard raises success from 186/216 to 205/216 (+8.80 points, 95% interval [3.70, 13.89]). The completeness check's benefit is unresolved: without it the agent scores 210/216 under ordinary responses and 204/216 under capped ones, against 205/216 with it.
- Drift, three ways (Section 5.4; Appendix G): a provider-free perturbation study (144 paired groups) and a recorded-replay study (89 records) are null, zero wrong answers in every arm; the continuation-graded study is adverse, 39/60 wrong in both arms. The verifier bought abstention only.
- Learned gate: the reviewed Table 23 comparison also changed the risk budget and grid, so it attributes nothing to the gate alone; no claim rests on it. Full statistical, support-only and deterministic routing are identical on all 2,724 record-model cases of the AWM comparison (Section 5.4; Appendix K), and the gate score is constant within each family. Section 3, Stage 6, presents the score as implemented, every admitted operating point as full coverage, and graded selection as not claimed.

Still open: the completeness check's benefit and protection under natural drift.

Clarification on calibration evidence: how are the calibration labels generated, and does calibration sampling precede filtering on baseline success or on the mined region?

Answer: for the primary families, by replaying existing trajectories, after filtering; the two newer end-to-end studies execute the continuation and sample before filtering.

- Primary labels: src/guarded_agentic_compaction/grc/compile.py::_calibration_samples runs the frozen program over each calibration group's recorded tool results and, after the verifier accepts, compares outputs with the recorded ones; the continuation never runs (Appendix C, "Calibration labels as computed").
- Filtering: discovery runs were filtered on exact traces before the 16/8/92 split (two issue-type runs and one PR run removed), so calibration sampling followed filtering. All three excluded records pass the saved guard and gate, so the primary bounds do not cover the unfiltered deployment population; Section 4 and Appendix G (396 of 396 records) say so, and the paper withdraws that claim.
- End-to-end studies: the post-cutoff study (132 PR groups, 0 violations, 90% bound 0.0173; Section 5.4) and the multi-repository study (Appendix F.2; registered stage 3 violations in 760 groups, U = 0.0133) run the frozen prefix, let the model continue and grade the final contract; the second samples one pull request per author before any filter.

Clarification on task requirements: the primary-task specification and which decisions need semantic or adaptive reasoning.

Answer: none does. The specification is in Table 16 and Appendix E.

Each sample is one issue or PR number from the pinned huggingface/datasets snapshot; the agent makes read-only calls and returns exact, source-grounded fields. Issue type: record, labels, comments; returns title, state, a label-derived category and a verbatim excerpt. PR outcome: record, merge status, discussion; returns open, merged or closed_unmerged, title and a comment excerpt. Backlog attention: open-issue record, assignees, discussion; returns owned, discussed_unowned or awaiting_first_response, title, owner and an excerpt. The model's remaining role is reading the evidence and writing the fields.

Summary of the changes: (1) the contribution is restated as safe automation of the program an engineer would write; (2) an eleven-repository end-to-end study grades final answers; (3) one-mechanism and drift studies were added; (4) the learned-gate claim is withdrawn; (5) BIRD and the adapted AWM comparison were added; (6) the adaptive-task attempts and gates are disclosed; (7) the compiler funnel and fresh-issue pilot (Appendix N).

We are glad to answer anything further.
