Paper Summary:
This study introduces guarded agentic compaction: a repeated, initially read-only sequence of agent actions may be converted into a deterministic program once checks on provenance, side effects, contracts, and calibration evidence have passed. Across three GitHub workflow families, the authors observe exact agreement with all 90 held-out contracts and 66.6% fewer model requests. Experiments on external traces also document cases in which the method declines compilation because the available calibration evidence is inadequate.

Critical Strengths:
The admission criteria address distinct sources of error, including arguments without supporting evidence, unintended effects, execution beyond the allowed prefix, and disagreement with the output contract. The examples involving issues #4420 and #6602 illustrate why these checks matter (Figures 1–3).
The empirical account includes both admitted programs and rejected candidates, together with their resource implications. Table 1 relates exact contract agreement to cost, and Table 2 demonstrates that recurring patterns in outside traces may still lack enough observations for admission.
Critical Weaknesses:
The broad safety language is stronger than the reported statistical result. According to Section 5.1, calibrating two candidates in each of two main families yields a selection-adjusted upper bound of 0.0569, which misses the 0.05 goal. Section 7 further finds that using creation day or author as the independent unit eliminates every family. The main claims should state these limits directly; additional independent calibration data would also make the evidence more persuasive.
The evidence does not yet show that the learned gate assigns higher scores to genuinely safer cases. At 92 calibration groups, the analysis in Section 3 and Figure 6 permits certification only at zero or complete coverage under the selected bound; the forward-in-time experiment likewise certifies no intermediate level. Testing a larger cohort that supports several certified coverage levels, and comparing with a gate based only on support at equal coverage, would clarify the contribution of the learned score.
Questions For Authors:
Does any main or supplementary cohort contain enough independent units to establish a 5% bound for the entire candidate-selection process? If none does, how will the abstract and conclusion be revised to reflect that limitation?
Could you compare the learned gate with a support-based gate on one held-out population at two or more positive, certified coverage levels? Such evidence would be important for evaluating the selective component.
